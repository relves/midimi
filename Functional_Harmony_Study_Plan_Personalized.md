# Personalized 8-Week Path: Functional Harmony & Music Creation

*Tailored for: RCM Grade ~4 piano background, recreational player, no formal scale training, limited notation reading.*

## Why This Version Is Different

Your background changes the plan in three important ways:

**Your advantage — keyboard facility.** Grade 4 RCM means your hands already work. You can play chords, move between them, and use both hands independently. Most theory learners struggle because their hands lag behind their understanding; yours won't. This plan leans into playing-based learning everywhere possible.

**Your advantage — you play by ear.** Learning recreationally without sight reading means you almost certainly developed informal ear skills. The ear-training components of this plan will come faster to you than to a paper-trained student. Trust your ear; this plan formalizes what it already half-knows.

**Your gap — scales and keys.** Never learning scales means you likely don't have instant recall of key signatures or which notes belong to which key. This matters because **diatonic harmony (Week 4 onward) assumes you know your major scales cold.** This plan patches that gap early with a daily micro-practice — and importantly, this is *scale spelling*, not scale technique. You don't need fast fingers; you need to know that A major contains F♯, C♯, and G♯ without thinking.

**About notation:** This plan does not require sight reading. Letter names are sufficient throughout. You had some notation exposure in RCM, and it may slowly come back — fine if it does, no problem if it doesn't. Don't let it become a barrier or a detour.

Core spine:

> **Scales (patch) → Intervals → Chords → Function → Motion → Creation**

## Week 0 — Setup + Scale Bootstrapping (3–4 days)

### Tools

- Your piano/keyboard
- Notebook or notes app
- Phone voice memos for recording yourself
- [musictheory.net](https://www.musictheory.net) for drills

### The Scale Patch (start immediately, continue daily)

This is your most important addition. **5 minutes daily, every day, for the entire 8 weeks:**

1. Learn the **circle of fifths** as an organizing map: C → G → D → A → E → B (sharps adding one at a time), and C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭ (flats adding one at a time).
2. Each day, pick **one key** and do the following:
   - Say its notes aloud, in order (e.g., "D major: D, E, F♯, G, A, B, C♯").
   - Play the scale slowly, one hand, while saying each note name. Speed is irrelevant — naming is everything.
   - Identify the sharps or flats in that key.
3. Rotation: start with C, G, F, D, B♭, A, E♭ (the keys you'll use most), then expand outward.

**Memory aid:** Each sharp key adds one sharp, and the new sharp is always a half-step below the key name (D major's new sharp is C♯). Each flat key adds one flat, and the new flat is the 4th note of the scale.

### Completion Criteria for Week 0

- Recite the notes of C, G, D, and F major without playing them.
- Explain what the circle of fifths is and why keys are arranged that way.

## Week 1 — Intervals as the Primitive

### Goal

Distinguish clearly between scale degrees, intervals, and chord tones. Begin formalizing your ear.

### Topics

- Letter distance vs semitone distance
- Interval numbers (2nd, 3rd, 5th, 7th)
- Interval quality: Major, Minor, Perfect, Augmented, Diminished
- Enharmonic spelling (F♯ vs G♭) and why correct spelling matters

### Daily Practice (20–30 min + 5 min scale patch)

1. Choose a root at random (start with notes from keys you've patched: C, G, D, F).
2. **Sing** each interval up from the root *before* playing it. As an ear player, you may find you can already do this better than you expect — this exercise names what you already hear.
3. Play and confirm:
   - Major 3rd
   - Minor 3rd
   - Perfect 5th
   - Perfect 4th
   - Minor 7th
4. musictheory.net interval drills for speed.

### Leveraging Your Background

Try this: play a melody from a song you used to play recreationally. Pick two consecutive notes and name the interval between them. Connecting interval names to music you already know in your hands accelerates everything.

### Completion Criteria

- Instantly identify intervals from any root.
- Explain *why* D → F♯ is a major 3rd without referencing a key.
- Sing a major 3rd and perfect 5th from a given note with reasonable accuracy.

## Week 2 — Triads (Chord DNA)

### Goal

Decode triads mechanically through interval stacking. You've played chords for years — this week explains what your hands already know.

### Topics

- Triad formulas:
  - Major: 1–3–5
  - Minor: 1–♭3–5
  - Diminished: 1–♭3–♭5
  - Augmented: 1–3–♯5
- Inversions (you may already use these without knowing their names)
- Why the 3rd defines chord quality
- Spelling matters: E♭–G♭–B♭ vs E♭–F♯–B♭

### Daily Practice (+5 min scale patch)

1. Pick a random root. Build all four triad types.
2. Play each in at least two inversions.
3. **Speak the interval formula aloud** as you play.
4. Eyes closed: have a chord app (or your own random reach) play a triad — major or minor? Your recreational ear likely gets this right already; now attach the *why*.

### Leveraging Your Background

Take a song you used to play. Identify the chords you were playing and name their quality and inversion. The phrase "oh, *that's* what that was" is the sound of this week working.

### Completion Criteria

- Instantly spell any major or minor triad from any root.
- Explain why E♭–G♭–B♭ is E♭ minor, not E♭ major.
- Identify major vs minor triads by ear reliably.

## Week 3 — Seventh Chords & the Meaning of "7"

### Goal

Internalize why "7" defaults to a minor 7th, and hear the tension seventh chords create.

### Topics

- Seventh chord formulas:
  - **maj7:** 1–3–5–7
  - **7 (dominant):** 1–3–5–♭7
  - **m7:** 1–♭3–5–♭7
  - **m(maj7):** 1–♭3–5–7
  - **ø7 (half-diminished):** 1–♭3–♭5–♭7
- Why "dominant 7" is the default meaning of "7"
- The dominant 7th's built-in tension

### Daily Practice (+5 min scale patch)

1. Build all five seventh chord types on one root per day.
2. Omit the 5th and listen — the 3rd and 7th define the chord.
3. **Side-by-side comparison:** Cmaj7 → Fmaj7, then C7 → F. The dominant 7th *pulls*; the maj7 floats. With your ear background, this contrast should land quickly.
4. Record yourself playing each type; listen back the next day and identify them.

### Completion Criteria

- Hear maj7 vs dominant 7 reliably.
- Explain why "Cmaj" does not imply a major 7th.
- Build any seventh chord from any root in under 10 seconds.

## ✦ Checkpoint — Foundations Review (1–2 days)

Before functional harmony, verify the foundation. **For you, the scale patch is part of this checkpoint.**

### Self-Assessment

1. **Scale check (critical for you):** Recite the notes of C, G, D, A, F, B♭, and E♭ major without playing. If any are shaky, do not proceed — Week 4 depends entirely on this.
2. **Interval speed:** 10 random roots → major 3rd, minor 3rd, perfect 5th, minor 7th from each. Target: under 5 seconds per interval.
3. **Chord spelling:** Every seventh chord type from E♭, F♯, and B♭.
4. **Ear check:** Identify intervals and chord qualities by sound alone.

If shaky, take 2–3 extra days. The plan has slack built in for exactly this.

## Week 4 — Diatonic Harmony (Chords in Keys)

### Goal

Understand where chords come from inside a key. This is where your scale patch pays off directly.

### Topics

- Harmonizing the major scale in 7ths
- Roman numerals: **Imaj7 – ii7 – iii7 – IVmaj7 – V7 – vi7 – viiø7**
- Why ii–V–I is structurally inevitable
- Tonic, predominant, dominant function (introduction)

### Daily Practice (+5 min scale patch)

1. Harmonize one major key per day — **start with your patched keys** (C, G, F, D), then expand.
2. Play with:
   - Left hand: root notes
   - Right hand: 3rd and 7th only (the essential voicing skill)
3. **Real music listening:** Pick songs you know — especially ones you used to play. Identify even one or two chords by function ("that's the V going home to I"). Your recreational repertoire is a goldmine here: you already know how these songs *feel*; now name why.

### Completion Criteria

- Instantly list the diatonic seventh chords in any patched key.
- Identify tonic, predominant, and dominant functions.
- Play 3rd-and-7th voicings for ii–V–I in at least four keys.

## Week 5 — Functional Harmony (Why Chords Move)

### Goal

Stop chaining chords randomly. Understand harmonic motion as tension and release — something your ear already feels.

### Topics

- Harmonic functions:
  - **Tonic** (I, iii, vi) — stability, home
  - **Predominant** (ii, IV) — motion away from home
  - **Dominant** (V, viiø) — tension demanding resolution
- Cadences:
  - **Authentic** (V → I): full resolution
  - **Half** (→ V): suspended tension
  - **Deceptive** (V → vi): expectation subverted
  - **Plagal** (IV → I): gentle close
- Why V resolves to I: the tritone inside V7 resolves by step into I

### Common Progression Vocabulary

Play each and listen for the functional logic — several of these are likely in songs you've already played:

- **I – vi – IV – V** (the "50s progression": T → T → PD → D)
- **I – IV – V – I** (the simplest functional cycle)
- **iii – vi – ii – V** (circle-of-fifths motion — each chord pulls to the next)
- **I – Imaj7 – I7 – IV** (line cliché — one voice descends chromatically)
- **ii – V – I** (the essential cadence)

### Daily Practice (+5 min scale patch)

1. Write 4-bar progressions using **T → PD → D → T**.
2. Play them; listen for inevitability at the resolution.
3. Analyze 2–3 songs from your old recreational repertoire with Roman numerals. Write them down.
4. Experiment: resolve V to vi instead of I. Notice the feeling — you've heard this trick in countless songs.

### Completion Criteria

- Predict likely resolutions when listening.
- Explain *why* V resolves to I (reference the tritone).
- Write a convincing 4–8 bar progression from scratch.

## Week 6 — Voice Leading (Horizontal Hearing)

### Goal

Hear harmony as moving lines, not vertical blocks. Your keyboard facility makes this week far more accessible to you than to most learners.

### Topics

- Guide tones (3rds and 7ths) as the skeleton of harmony
- Tritone resolution: the 3rd of V becomes the root of I; the 7th of V becomes the 3rd of I
- Smooth voice leading: minimal motion between chords
- Why roots are often omitted in voicings

### Daily Practice (+5 min scale patch)

1. Play ii–V–I using **only 3rds and 7ths**, starting in patched keys, expanding toward all 12.
2. Watch the voices: one stays, one steps down. That's voice leading.
3. **★ Highest-priority exercise in the entire course:** Sing one voice while playing the other. Then switch. As someone who learned by ear, you may take to this unusually fast — and it develops harmonic hearing faster than anything else here.
4. Add roots in the left hand once guide tones feel comfortable.

### Completion Criteria

- Smooth guide-tone ii–V–I in at least 8 keys (all 12 is the stretch goal).
- Explain why roots are often omitted.
- Hear resolution through the 3rds and 7ths alone.

## Weeks 7–8 — Composition & Analysis

### Goal

Apply everything to actual music creation — the reason you're doing this.

### Week 7: Reharmonization

Take a melody from your old recreational repertoire — something already in your fingers. Harmonize it three ways:

1. Diatonic chords in expected functions.
2. Substitute at least one functional equivalent (vi for I, iii for V).
3. Add a deceptive cadence or a chromatic passing chord.

Hearing a song you've played for years transform under new harmony is one of the most motivating experiences in music learning.

### Week 8: Original Composition

Write a 16-bar piece (or verse + chorus) with:

- Clear stability (tonic function)
- Deliberate tension (predominant → dominant)
- Satisfying resolution
- One moment that subverts expectation

Analyze your own piece with Roman numerals. Can you justify every choice?

### Ongoing: Song Analysis

Keep a notebook of Roman-numeral analyses of songs you love. Patterns will emerge — those patterns are the real curriculum.

## Book Recommendation (Adjusted for You)

### Primary: The Jazz Theory Book — Mark Levine

For your profile, this is the clear choice over Kostka & Payne:

- **Kostka & Payne (Tonal Harmony)** is excellent but heavily notation-based — it assumes fluent staff reading throughout. Given that sight reading isn't your strength and isn't required for your goals, it would create friction without adding value.
- **Levine** works from chord symbols and keyboard thinking, treats chords as living functional objects, and aligns with how recreational players actually approach music. Some examples use notation, but the concepts are graspable at the keyboard.

### Optional Companion

**Harmony and Voice Leading — Aldwell & Schachter** — only if Weeks 5–6 leave you hungry for more depth, and only as a selective reference. It is notation-heavy; don't buy it preemptively.

### Interactive Resource

**[musictheory.net](https://www.musictheory.net)** — free interval, chord, scale, and ear drills. The scale exercises directly support your daily scale patch.

## Scope Clarification

Not required at this stage:

- Modes
- Extended harmony (9ths, 11ths, 13ths)
- Counterpoint
- Orchestration
- Secondary dominants and modulation
- **Sight reading** — explicitly deferred. It is a separate skill, valuable but independent of functional harmony. If you want it later, pursue it after Week 8 as its own project.

By the end of Week 8:
